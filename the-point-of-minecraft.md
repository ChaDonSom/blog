# The point of Minecraft

[Sansbury, Jacob: Optimizing the point away](https://www.workbyjacob.com/thoughts/optimizing-the-point-away)​

I came across that article by Mr. Sansbury recently and it caught my attention. He doesn't necessarily fit in lock-step with my thoughts, but his perspective shows some things I've been thinking about in an interesting light. 

I think his main point was something like this: People don't know how to make games anymore. His argument is sound, that, essentially anything you can measure about a game's success, is only a weak proxy of its true success. You can measure how many people it sells to - but what happens if you just sacrifice the valuable difficulty to make it easy enough for the masses?

But I wanted to take his perspective and use it to dive into my own ruminations on Minecraft of late, which I think are related.

One thing he says about Minecraft is how its point (purpose) was saved by Notch removing the auto-created wooden house at spawn.

I want to explore the implied purpose of Minecraft, there. When he says removing the house saved its purpose, you can guess he means Notch made players go exploring. But the more I investigate Minecraft, I come to this conclusion: its purpose is not discovery, or at least, discovery its not its main purpose but a secondary part of it.

Also, I think things have a purpose given to them from God, that may or may not be what their creators actually intended. It's like how Oppenheimer considered the atomic bomb - the ripples of this thing are impossible to know, so whatever our purpose with it, what will its true nature reveal?. Minecraft's "true purpose", my theory of it, anyway, might have been lost on Notch and anyone else who created it; but I do think that some of them knew what it was, but don't want that to be the case.

I should explain why my theory is only a theory, though: I left Minecraft years ago, along with all video games, because I could not figure out what any game's purpose was in my life. When I learned to program, the complexity of code showed me that virtually no lessons could be reliably taken from a video game to the real world. Over time, I've started to recover some ways to learn valuable lessons again: thinking of video games as stories the developers are telling, helps somewhat. But the default is still skepticism: for any new game, I basically have to assume it's unrelated to anything that matters.

So, what follows is my theory for Minecraft, less a settled declaration than an attempt to put my finger on why it can still feel purposeful, after that long absence.

You can see its "true purpose" in what the most popular thing to do with it is: building.

Not exploring.

When Minecraft was newly released, I remember the "big surprise" it carried, as opposed to the rest of the video games at the time, was that you could modify the entire world. Nothing was static. I remember how Crysis felt, how you could tear apart every building and chop down every tree. That was a feat, and it's still not the norm, because video games don't work that way. To make things run nicely, it has to be simple.

The first time I tried Minecraft, I left frustrated because I couldn't figure out you had to hold the mouse button down to keep hitting things until they broke.

It was only the promise of building and terraforming that brought me back.

Now, people will say that the point of Minecraft is not building, because, although some like to play in creative mode and just build things, it's nothing like a majority of players. I admit that. But I think pure creative mode isn't a true signal for whether people like building.

I've built a few medium-scale projects in creative in Minecraft. They all suffer from the same problem. Once you've built it, what do you do with it?

You can build a castle. Now what? You could wander the halls - the halls you made already and already intimately know every block? You could populate it with villagers - simple automatons that only know "bed", "trade block", and "player trade"?

You could... all you can do is... look at it.

This is why creative mode, though "purely" closer to Minecraft's purpose, fails. You can build with Legos, but if all you have is 2x4 bricks... While only 2x4 bricks is the most capable construction medium with Legos, there's surprisingly little you can do with them. They don't _mean_ anything without windows, minifigs, vehicles, and garage doors.

Minecraft's creative mode doesn't mean anything. In architecture, form follows function. If you built a castle in creative mode, what was it defending against? If you built an orchard, what were its products for? The very identity of a structure depends on its purpose. The best you can do in creative mode is a lookalike, a drawing, a sketch.

And anything you make in Minecraft, for purely aesthetic reasons, is uncontestedly worse aesthetically, than virtually any other medium you could use - unless you accept blockiness as a deliberate constraint.

But: when there are game elements that prevent you from building if you don't maintain them, now there's a "function" the form can follow. It will be irrevocably removed from the "pure" sketch you can make in creative mode - there are no bathrooms in Minecraft, even if you make something that looks like one - but it will have at least _something_​ that's a little more native to this novel idea of voxels you can add or remove. You can build something you could call "orchards". You can start to rediscover the concept of "rooms" in buildings.

When you have a limited personal inventory, lava pools can be useful as trash cans.

But this is what I think the creators and maintainers of Minecraft are struggling with today: the "survival" mode, and other modes and "side quests" they're adding to the game, can only ever be supplementary to the main purpose. The best projects you build in Minecraft are the ones that make your "survival" game-mechanics-balancing easier.

Because high-level enchanting requires leather in vast quantities, an ugly square of fences holding in 50 cows is quite valuable. You'll visit it far more often than a gazebo you built overlooking some vast meadow, where the meadow's resolution is limited to a meter and the most that can happen is tall grass pretending to blow in the wind, and sheep wandering aimlessly.

But that cow pen - if you add just a few gate posts and a little flourishes here and there - the payoff will be there with you, where you spend your most time.

So in the end, the success of Minecraft was that there's a medium, voxels, that could be interesting, but it's incredibly ugly - it is aesthetically beat by anything else at all - painting, drawing, video, tracing lines in the sand at the beach. But, if you make an incentive structure, things can take value around it.

This is like economy and capitalism. Enormous structure is built up around a simple concept, which concept is more like a "game of life" seed than a manual.

As of late, Minecraft has gone in a direction that feels more like "quests". "Here's another thing you can find, it's a building this or that big and has that kind of NPC in it". Yet, without being intrinsically tied to the core "economy" of the game, that new exploration, like the act of building is in "creative mode", is meaningless.

I still don't know if the incentive structure is really what makes building in Minecraft valuable - building lessons still virtually never map to the real world - but I do think we value what we earn, and throw away what we don't. If the developers of Minecraft want it to keep capturing people's hearts and minds, I'd say that fragile economic connector between building and its cost - that's what they need to preserve.
